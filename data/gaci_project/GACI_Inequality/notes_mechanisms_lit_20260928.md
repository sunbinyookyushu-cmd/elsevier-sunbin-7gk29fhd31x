# Mechanisms: why hub connectivity raises within-country inequality — literature (verified via refcheck, 2026-09-28)

All references below were verified against Crossref/Semantic Scholar (verdict "verified"). Years corrected where the online-first year differs.

## Channel 1. Face-to-face access raises demand for skilled, hub-city occupations (skill-biased demand)
- Campante & Yanagizawa-Drott, "Long-Range Growth: Economic Development in the Global Network of Air Links", QJE 133(3), 2018 (online 2017), doi 10.1093/qje/qjx050. Long-haul links raise business links and capital flows and local economic activity near the connected airport; gains are spatially concentrated around hubs.
- Giroud, "Proximity and Investment: Evidence from Plant-Level Data", QJE 128(2), 2013, doi 10.1093/qje/qjs073. New airline routes that cut HQ-plant travel time raise plant investment and productivity: connectivity is an input to managerial/professional work.
- Bernstein, Giroud & Townsend, "The Impact of Venture Capital Monitoring", J Finance 71(4), 2016, doi 10.1111/jofi.12370. Direct flights raise VC involvement, innovation and exits: connectivity complements high-skill finance.
- Cristea, "Buyer-seller relationships in international trade: Evidence from U.S. States' exports and business-class travel", JIE 84(2), 2011, doi 10.1016/j.jinteco.2011.02.003. Business-class travel is an input to exporting differentiated goods.
- Hovhannisyan & Keller, "International business travel: an engine of innovation?", J Econ Growth 20(1), 2015 (online 2014), doi 10.1007/s10887-014-9107-7. Inbound business travel raises patenting.
- Catalini, Fons-Rosen & Gaulé, "How Do Travel Costs Shape Collaboration?", Management Science 66(8), 2020, doi 10.1287/mnsc.2019.3381. Cheaper air travel raises scientific collaboration.
- Sheard, "Airports and urban sectoral employment", JUE 80, 2014, doi 10.1016/j.jue.2014.01.002. Airport size raises employment in tradable services, not manufacturing.
- Blonigen & Cristea, "Air service and urban growth", JUE 86, 2015, doi 10.1016/j.jue.2015.02.001. Air service growth raises metro population, income and employment, concentrated in service sectors.
Reading for the paper: the direct beneficiaries of a hub are the occupations that use face-to-face contact (management, finance, professional services, R&D); these sit in the upper half of the distribution. Hence income elasticities above the mean for p60-100 and below the mean for the bottom half.

## Channel 2. Agglomeration and spatial sorting around the hub city (urban premium, urban-rural gap)
- Diamond, "The Determinants and Welfare Implications of US Workers' Diverging Location Choices by Skill", AER 106(3), 2016, doi 10.1257/aer.20131706. Skilled workers sort into high-amenity, high-productivity cities; sorting amplifies wage inequality.
- Gaubert, "Firm Sorting and Agglomeration", AER 108(11), 2018, doi 10.1257/aer.20150361. More productive firms sort into larger cities; gains from agglomeration accrue disproportionately there.
- Fajgelbaum & Gaubert, "Optimal Spatial Policies, Geography, and Sorting", QJE 135(2), 2020, doi 10.1093/qje/qjaa001. Spatial sorting by skill is inefficiently high; transport improvements that favour the largest city amplify it.
- Baum-Snow, Freedman & Pavan, "Why Has Urban Inequality Increased?", AEJ Applied 10(4), 2018, doi 10.1257/app.20160510. Inequality grew faster in larger cities; agglomeration complements skill.
- Moretti, "Real Wage Inequality", AEJ Applied 5(1), 2013, doi 10.1257/app.5.1.65. Nominal skill premia are partly absorbed by housing costs in high-wage cities; relevant caveat for real-income interpretation.
- Young, "Inequality, the Urban-Rural Gap, and Migration", QJE 128(4), 2013, doi 10.1093/qje/qjt025. In developing countries roughly 40 percent of measured inequality is the urban-rural gap.
Reading for the paper: a national hub is located in the primate city; connectivity growth is a city-specific productivity shock that widens the urban premium and the urban-rural gap. Consistent with the urbanisation median split (effect only above the median) and with the strongest effects in countries with a single dominant airport (top-airport share tercile).

## Channel 3. Trade/FDI exposure and the skill premium (globalisation channel)
- Feenstra & Hanson, "Foreign direct investment and relative wages: Evidence from Mexico's maquiladoras", JIE 42(3-4), 1997, doi 10.1016/s0022-1996(96)01475-4. FDI raises the relative demand for skilled labour in the receiving country.
- Helpman, Itskhoki, Muendler & Redding, "Trade and Inequality: From Theory to Estimation", REStud 84(1), 2017 (online 2016), doi 10.1093/restud/rdw025. Exporting raises wage dispersion through firm heterogeneity; the connectivity-trade link is the companion paper's result.
- Kleven, Landais & Saez, "Taxation and International Migration of Superstars", AER 103(5), 2013, doi 10.1257/aer.103.5.1892. Top earners are internationally mobile; hubs lower the cost of that mobility (top-1% channel; our top-share estimates are imprecise, so cite as background only).
Reading for the paper: the companion trade paper shows hub connectivity raises trade and FDI; the trade-inequality literature predicts a skill premium from that exposure. This is the channel behind "upper half grows with the mean, bottom half less".

## Channel 4. Tourism (the counter-channel)
- Faber & Gaubert, "Tourism and Economic Development: Evidence from Mexico's Coastline", AER 109(6), 2019, doi 10.1257/aer.20161434. Tourism raises local wages and employment including in low-skill services, with positive spillovers to manufacturing.
Reading for the paper: leisure connectivity could compress the distribution; the tourism-heritage instrument was dropped, but the channel remains a reason the effect is stage-dependent (thin networks: shared growth; take-off hubs: business/skill-biased).

## How the evidence lines up with our heterogeneity
- Effect absent in the thinnest networks, present at take-off: consistent with Channels 1-2 needing a functioning hub city (fixed-cost/agglomeration threshold).
- Effect present above the urbanisation median, absent below: Channel 2.
- Effect strongest where one airport dominates national connectivity: Channels 1-2 (gains concentrated in one city).
- No spillover from neighbours' hubs: the mechanism is domestic and local.
- Effect weaker/absent in already open economies (trade/GDP above median): Channel 3 saturates.

## What we cannot show
