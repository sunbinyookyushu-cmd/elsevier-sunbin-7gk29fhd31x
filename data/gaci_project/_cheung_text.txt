ABSTRACT
This paper studies the evolution of global air transport network over the period 2006–2016. A
Global Airport Connectivity Index (GACI) is proposed, combining degree, closeness and eigen-
vectortopologicalindicatorsandtwonewvolumetricindicators.Wefindfasterpassengergrowth
for larger airports that have improved in GACI. While America and North Asia focused on re-
gional hub developments, serving short- to medium-haul routes, West Europe, South East Asia
and Middle East focused on developing their major airports into global hubs. Our analysis shows
that increasing GACI improves an airport’s competitive position and gives rise to increased in-
fluence over other airports.
1. Introduction
Air transport has both contributed to and benefited from increased participation in the global economy that has helped many
cities in the world become deeply connected to close trading partners. The direct economic benefits from aviation are palpable. It is
estimated that the air transport sector directly employed 10 million people and generated US$3.0 trillion in value-added output
globally (ATAG, 2018). Continued demand for air transport, together with ongoing opening up of the skies, provided incentive for
airport-cities to further expand their connectivity. The number of unique city-pair connections exceeded 18,400 in 2016, a 4%
increase from the previous year and a near doubling of the connections 20 years ago (IATA, 2017). The measure of connectivity has
become a key indicator of the economic importance of a city in the global air transport network (IATA, 2019; Arvis and Shepherd,
2013). Higher level of air connectivity is said to have greater potential in creating advantages for employment growth, delivering
economic and social benefits to the area (e.g.,Irwin and Kasarda, 1991; Pearce, 2007; Bel and Fageda, 2008; Zhang, 2012; Florida
et al., 2015). Consequently, there is a significant interest to analyse air transport networks in terms of connections. The study of air
connectivity is also an important tool to assist stakeholders’ forecast and planning. It helps benchmark and monitor the network
performance against that of other airports or airlines (Burghouwt and Redondi, 2013). Industry leaders or policy makers can make
use of the study findings to identify major competition and cooperation opportunities (Burghouwt and Veldhuis, 2006). The results
may, for instance, provide a clearer picture of destinations and airport requirements needed to attract airlines to operate new
international routes.
Understanding the topology of air transport network is important for two primary reasons. First, the global connectivity by air is
growing larger and more complex. Connectivity represents a momentous proxy in understanding passengers’ route choice, including

2. Data and metrics
2.1. Data preparation
Our flight data comes from the Official Airline Guide database (OAG, 2019), which offers a comprehensive coverage of ap-
proximately 1000 airline schedules. Specifically, route segment data is used in this study. It gives weekly passenger seat capacity of
allthescheduledflightservicesaroundtheworld.Thedatasetcoversover3500airportsand60,000unidirectionalroutesworldwide
overan11-yearperiodbetween2006and2016.Segmentinformationisthenaggregatedoveralltheflightsbetweeneveryconnected
airport pair, in each year. Other flight information recorded in the segment includes origin and destination airports, operating
carriers, and flight frequency. The data set also contains airport location information on city, country, and region.
To focus on scheduled commercial air transport services, four types of airports are excluded in this study: (i) Military airports,
such as air force base; (ii) Airports for private jets, charters, seasonal or seaplanes only; (iii) Heliports; and (iv) Airports that were
closed between 2006 and 2016. A summary of the air transport networks in 2006, 2011 and 2016 is given inTable 1. While the
number of airports has a moderate increase of around 6%, the number of direct flight routes and total capacity have both increased
substantially by more than 25% and 44%, respectively, over the 11-year period. Furthermore, density has increased, indicating that
more direct linkages are formed between smaller airports. This is in part also supported by the reduction in diameter of the network
and the gain in average degree over the same period.

4. Global airport connectivity index (GACI) evolution 2006–2016
One of the basic questions about air transport network is the identification of important airports worldwide. As global passenger
flows become more concentrated in the hub cities and at the same time, more dispersed to secondary airports within those cities
(Wong et al., 2019a), some secondary airports have risen in their global positions in the air transport network. While hub airports
remain more connected than ever, emerging markets are gaining share of total inbound and outbound travellers. Some of the main
airports in emerging markets have started to surpass the traditional hubs in global importance. We have developed a Global Airport
Connectivity Index (GACI), which measures each airport’s relative importance in the air transport network, by combing the five
selected centrality indicators into a single score. It helps identify and assess dominant airports as to where they rank in relation to
their counterparts, upon changes of network topology and passenger flows through the network over time. The weight associated
with each indicator is tuned in a specific way which (i) retains the most valuable parts of all of the indicators, and (ii) gauges the
potential scale of future passenger flows to facilitate comparison of GACI over time.

6. Concluding remarks
Thispaperhas analysedthe nature andevolution of the worldairtransportnetwork bydeveloping andapplying aGlobal Airport
Connectivity Index (GACI). This GACI has incorporated the topology and volumetric characteristics of an airport, as well as its
regionalimportanceintheglobalairtransportnetwork.Morespecifically,theGACIcombinesexistingcentralityindicatorsincluding
degree, closeness and eigenvector, together with two new volume-based indicators: flow betweenness and regional importance. The
flow-betweenness measures the importance of an airport as the transit point on connection-paths between pairs of origin and des-
tination.Regional importancemeasures thelink intensityof an airport’sconnectionswithinits region.Together,these fivecentrality
indicators captured a broad range of air transport network specific properties, such as power law degree distribution, hubness,
preferred within-region connections, faster growth in passenger capacity of larger airports, short mean path length, logarithmical
shrinkage in network size over time, and distinct regional patterns of global passenger flows. By computing the GACI changes over
the years, we can evaluate the significance of network developments due to changes induced by new connections to the airports.
The analysis of the historical seat capacity data of 3500 airports, together with the airports’ complex network structure, revealed
deeper insights in the developments of air transport network over time. First, the air transport network is not scale-free. Majority of
thecapacitygrowthconcentratedatthemega-degree(r≥200)airports,whichachievedpassengercapacitycompoundgrowthabout
four times faster than those smaller airports. An airport with a high closeness centrality score tends to have a high capacity volume.
From the flow-betweenness performance, we found that most Middle-East and Chinese airports were on the rise and some of them
have overtaken the traditional hubs in Europe and North America over this study period. Instead of using the traditional US and
Europeanairportsfortransit,alargenumberofpassengersswitchedtouseMiddle-EastandAsianairports.Lastly,withtheexception
ofNorthAmerica,alltheotherregionshadimprovedtheireigenvectorcentralityscores,withMiddle-EastandAsiaairportshadmore
than doubled their average scores over the same period. These airports are getting very prominent in the air transport network.
The GACI was calculated for every airport, and the evolution of air transport networks across all the world regions were studied.
First, different regions appeared to have different strategies in expanding their air transport networks. Both America and North Asia
focused on developing regional hub airports, serving short- to medium-haul routes to address regional demands. While for West
Europe,SouthEastAsiaandMiddleEast,theyseemedtoprefertodevelopingtheirmajorairportsintoglobalhubs,servinglong-haul
and international flights. Although the average GACI did not change much during this period, there was a fast growth in European
andAsiasecondaryairports.Tomaintainorimprovethehubpositionofanairportwithlargetrafficvolume,theairportmustdevelop
new routes connecting to higher GACI airports.
As airport competes with one another on passenger traffics and service quality to both airlines and passengers (Wiltshire, 2018),
the public utility status of airports has been fading and airports have been increasingly being operated like a business.Many airports
are under growing pressure to be more financially self-sufficient (e.g.Zhang and Zhang, 2003). For the regulators, they should find
ways to optimise the allocation of additional routes in a liberalising airline market, which can maximise social welfare and minimise
passengers’traveldisutility(Lietal.,2010).Tomanytravellers,airportandroutechoiceisalwaysajointdecision(Yangetal.,2014),
especially with long-haul journey and transit flights. These dynamic changes in aviation business environment encouraged the
vertical airport–airline relationships, in order to strengthen the hub status of major airports (Zhang and Czerny, 2012; Yang et al.,
2015). Many airports and airlines established close and long-term relationship to attain a win-win situation: with airports being able
to attract more passengers from existing (with higher frequency) and new routes (direct flights), substantial profits can be generated
from the terminal, including those from leasing areas used for catering and retailing. The findings in this paper shed some lights to
policy makers and industry leaders in planning their aviation strategies.
In the future, the application of GACI on airports and routes development can be further explored, particularly in the areas of
airportclassification andstrategic network change configuration pertinent to airport growth. A GACI-based airport classificationcan
add the connectivity dimension to many existing airport classification schemes that are often based on such factors as public access,
runway length, commercial services, and general aviation facilities. It can also precisely define the airport roles, such as super-hub,
major, regional, local and minor airport, in the regional and global aviation network. Furthermore, GACI can provide a measure of
the network changes to specific airports, assisting policy makers to understand, for instance, the effects of strategy route changes by
otherairports. The performance of an airport isaffected bynot onlyits local andregional economy,but alsoits connectivityto other
airports, as well as other airports’ connectivity. Being able to capture, examine and predict the temporal changes of the air transport
network, more insightful analyses can be performed so as to improve the competitiveness of an airport city. Indeed, a similar GACI
canbealsocomputedatanairport-cityleveltomeasuretheconnectivityandimportanceofanairport-cityintheglobalairtransport
network. This would represent a natural extension of the analysis presented in the present paper.
